# Early Prototype Construction Guide

## CORELINE Mechanical Prototype

This document describes the general construction approach used for early mechanical prototypes of the CORELINE Smart Spine Corset.

It is intended to support **repeatable prototyping, engineering evaluation, and design iteration**. It is not a final manufacturing specification, production assembly manual, or clinical fitting protocol.

> **Development status:** Early prototype / mechanical validation  
> Construction methods, component geometry, materials, attachment methods, and assembly sequences remain subject to change as testing progresses.

---

## Purpose

The early prototype construction process is intended to support evaluation of:

- overall mechanical architecture;
- structural support behavior;
- load distribution;
- flexibility and controlled movement;
- adjustment mechanisms;
- fit across different torso geometries;
- comfort and breathability;
- component placement;
- assembly feasibility; and
- design-for-manufacturing considerations.

The primary objective is to create testable prototype configurations while preserving the ability to modify individual design variables between iterations.

---

## Prototype Architecture

The mechanical prototype is based on a layered, body-contoured structure.

At a high level, the system may include:

1. a body-facing comfort layer;
2. structural textile panels;
3. reinforcement zones;
4. flexible or semi-rigid support elements;
5. load-transfer and compression elements;
6. adjustable straps or closures; and
7. protective finishing around edges and structural interfaces.

The exact configuration depends on the prototype revision.

No single prototype configuration should be treated as a final production architecture.

---

## Construction Principles

### 1. Modular development

Where practical, prototype components should remain replaceable or adjustable.

This allows individual variables to be tested without rebuilding the complete prototype.

Examples include:

- interchangeable structural elements;
- adjustable strap positions;
- removable reinforcement sections;
- replaceable padding;
- alternative closure configurations; and
- different material combinations.

---

### 2. Symmetrical baseline construction

Initial mechanical prototypes should normally use a symmetrical left/right configuration unless asymmetry is being intentionally investigated.

Symmetry simplifies evaluation of:

- structural behavior;
- tension distribution;
- component positioning;
- fit;
- manufacturing consistency; and
- changes between prototype revisions.

Any intentional asymmetry should be documented.

---

### 3. Progressive stiffness

The prototype should avoid unnecessary abrupt transitions between very soft and very rigid regions.

Where practical, structural transitions should be distributed through:

- layered textiles;
- reinforcement geometry;
- flexible interfaces;
- gradual changes in material stiffness; or
- controlled spacing between structural elements.

The objective is to reduce concentrated loads and improve mechanical integration between components.

---

### 4. Controlled adjustability

Adjustment mechanisms should provide sufficient range for prototype fitting while remaining repeatable.

Adjustment components should be positioned so that they can be:

- accessed easily;
- adjusted progressively;
- secured reliably;
- compared between tests; and
- replaced during development.

Maximum available tension should not be treated as the default fitting condition.

## Prototype Preparation

Before assembly:

1. identify the prototype revision;
2. verify the intended material configuration;
3. inspect materials for visible damage;
4. prepare panel patterns or templates;
5. mark structural reference lines;
6. mark attachment and reinforcement zones;
7. identify left and right components where necessary;
8. verify the intended orientation of stretch-sensitive materials; and
9. record any intentional deviations from the current prototype design.

Prototype changes should be documented before or immediately after construction.

---

## Panel Preparation

Textile panels should be prepared consistently so that different prototype revisions can be compared.

During cutting and preparation:

- maintain the intended material orientation;
- account for directional stretch where relevant;
- avoid damaged material areas;
- preserve sufficient edge allowance for the selected assembly method;
- clearly identify structural reference points; and
- keep paired left/right components geometrically consistent unless otherwise specified.

Prototype seam allowances may vary during development and should not be interpreted as final manufacturing tolerances.

---

## Layer Assembly

Where multiple layers are used, they should be aligned according to the intended mechanical function.

A typical development sequence may include:

1. body-facing layer;
2. cushioning or spacer layer where required;
3. structural textile layer;
4. local reinforcement;
5. structural-element interface;
6. external retention or cover layer; and
7. edge finishing.

Not every prototype requires every layer.

The layer sequence may be modified to evaluate different mechanical behaviors.

---

## Structural Support Elements

Flexible or semi-rigid support elements may be incorporated into dedicated channels, pockets, attachment zones, or other replaceable interfaces.

Their installation should allow evaluation of:

- longitudinal stiffness;
- lateral flexibility;
- torsional response;
- spacing;
- curvature;
- support-zone coverage;
- interaction with textile layers; and
- behavior during movement.

Where possible during early development, structural elements should remain removable so alternative materials or geometries can be tested.

---

## Structural Element Safety

Before installation, structural components should be inspected for:

- sharp edges;
- splinters;
- cracks;
- delamination;
- exposed fibers;
- deformation;
- damaged surfaces; and
- other defects that could damage the textile structure or contact the user.

Edges and ends should be appropriately protected within the prototype architecture.

Damaged structural components should not be used for wear testing.

---

## Reinforcement Zones

Local reinforcement may be added around:

- structural element interfaces;
- strap attachment points;
- closure zones;
- high-load seams;
- adjustment interfaces; and
- areas repeatedly modified during prototyping.

Reinforcement should distribute loads rather than simply increase local rigidity.

Excessive reinforcement may reduce flexibility and should therefore be evaluated experimentally.

---

## Adjustment and Closure System

Adjustment systems may use combinations of:

- webbing;
- textile straps;
- elastic sections;
- hook-and-loop fasteners;
- buckles or guides; and
- reinforced attachment points.

The prototype closure system should provide:

- repeatable adjustment;
- stable retention;
- controlled compression;
- adequate range;
- practical release; and
- minimal interference with normal movement.

Attachment points should be inspected regularly because prototype adjustment systems may experience repeated loading during testing.

---

## Strap Installation

During prototype assembly, straps should be positioned according to the intended load-transfer path.

Before permanent or semi-permanent attachment, verify:

- orientation;
- usable adjustment range;
- symmetry;
- interaction with adjacent components;
- freedom from twisting;
- accessibility while worn; and
- clearance from sensitive edge areas.

Where practical, early prototypes may use repositionable strap attachment methods to support iterative testing.

---

## Seams and Joining

The joining method should be appropriate for the materials and loads involved.

Prototype joining methods may include:

- conventional stitching;
- reinforced stitching;
- textile bonding;
- temporary attachment;
- removable fasteners; or
- combinations of these methods.

Selection depends on the purpose of the prototype iteration.

Load-bearing attachment points should receive particular attention during inspection.

---

## Edge Finishing

Edges that contact or approach the body should be finished to reduce:

- abrasion;
- localized pressure;
- material fraying;
- delamination;
- structural-element exposure; and
- discomfort during movement.

Potential solutions include:

- textile binding;
- folded textile edges;
- soft protective layers;
- enclosed seams; or
- other prototype-appropriate finishing methods.

Edge construction remains subject to comfort testing.

---

## Assembly Sequence

A typical early-prototype assembly sequence is:

1. define the prototype revision;
2. prepare patterns or dimensional references;
3. cut primary textile panels;
4. mark structural and adjustment reference locations;
5. prepare reinforcement zones;
6. assemble the primary textile layers;
7. create structural-element interfaces or channels;
8. install reinforcement components;
9. attach adjustment and closure components;
10. insert or install structural elements;
11. finish exposed edges;
12. inspect the complete assembly;
13. perform an initial unloaded mechanical check;
14. conduct controlled fitting;
15. document observations; and
16. record required changes for the next revision.

The sequence may be modified when a prototype is intended to test only a specific subsystem.

---

## Pre-Fit Inspection

Before a prototype is worn, inspect the complete assembly.

Check for:

- exposed sharp edges;
- damaged structural elements;
- loose components;
- incomplete seams;
- unstable attachment points;
- twisted straps;
- unintended material folds;
- excessive adhesive exposure where applicable;
- closure malfunction; and
- obvious left/right asymmetry.

Any defect that could affect safety or invalidate the test should be corrected before fitting.

---

## Initial Mechanical Check

Before wear testing, manually evaluate:

- overall flexibility;
- structural element retention;
- closure operation;
- strap travel;
- symmetry;
- panel movement;
- excessive local stiffness;
- material bunching; and
- obvious interference between components.

This check is intended to identify basic assembly problems before the prototype is placed under normal fitting loads.

---

## Prototype Fitting

Detailed fitting principles are documented in:

[`dimensions.md`](dimensions.md)

For initial construction verification:

1. position the prototype without high tension;
2. confirm basic anatomical alignment;
3. secure the primary closure;
4. apply adjustment progressively;
5. check structural component position;
6. confirm that normal breathing is unrestricted;
7. inspect edge contact;
8. evaluate sitting and standing;
9. perform basic controlled torso movement; and
10. stop the evaluation if the prototype shows structural failure or creates unacceptable discomfort.

The purpose of early fitting is engineering evaluation, not clinical treatment.

---

## Quality Checks

Each completed prototype should be checked for:

### Geometry

- left/right consistency;
- correct component orientation;
- intended panel alignment; and
- structural element positioning.

### Assembly

- seam integrity;
- reinforcement attachment;
- closure operation;
- strap attachment; and
- edge finishing.

### Mechanical behavior

- expected flexibility;
- controlled structural response;
- stable adjustment;
- absence of obvious component migration; and
- absence of unintended rigid pressure points.

### Documentation

- prototype revision recorded;
- material changes recorded;
- construction changes recorded; and
- observed issues documented.

## Iterative Development

Prototype development should preferably change a limited number of major variables at a time.

Examples include testing:

- one structural material against another;
- different structural element spacing;
- alternative reinforcement geometry;
- different strap positions;
- alternative textile combinations; or
- modified panel geometry.

Changing many major variables simultaneously can make it difficult to determine which modification caused an observed improvement or problem.

---

## Common Prototype Issues

Issues to monitor during development include:

- panel migration;
- strap slippage;
- uneven compression;
- excessive edge pressure;
- structural element movement;
- material bunching;
- excessive heat retention;
- local seam loading;
- restricted torso movement;
- inadequate adjustment range;
- asymmetric tension; and
- component wear.

Observed issues should be documented rather than corrected informally without recording the change.

---

## Design for Manufacturability

Hand-built prototypes are not equivalent to production-ready assemblies.

As development progresses, construction should be reviewed for:

- repeatable cutting;
- standardized component geometry;
- seam accessibility;
- assembly sequence;
- material availability;
- number of manufacturing operations;
- tooling requirements;
- quality-control checkpoints;
- component traceability;
- repairability;
- production tolerances; and
- manufacturing cost.

A design that performs well mechanically may still require significant changes before production.

---

## Future Sensor Integration

The core mechanical prototype is intended to function independently of active electronics.

Future versions may provide defined interfaces for optional sensor modules.

Any sensor integration should avoid unnecessary changes to the primary mechanical support architecture.

Potential integration considerations include:

- removable sensor pockets;
- cable or interconnect routing where applicable;
- protection of electronic components;
- access for charging or maintenance;
- separation from high-load structural interfaces; and
- removal of electronics for textile maintenance where required.

Sensor development is documented separately in the repository.

---

## Documentation Requirements

Each meaningful construction change should record:

- what changed;
- why it changed;
- which prototype revision was affected;
- materials involved;
- expected effect;
- observed result; and
- whether the change should be retained, modified, or rejected.

This approach helps convert iterative prototyping into reproducible engineering development.

The CORELINE mechanical architecture remains under active prototype development.

This document describes an **early-stage engineering prototype and development process**.
